# Replacing a person's identity keys

A person whose identity key may have leaked should be able to replace it with a new one, and have the people and spaces they share with move to the new key in an orderly way (#327). Today nothing replaces a person's keys. A key the server shows at a higher key version is a key change, with a warning that stops sharing until someone confirms it by phone or in person, and an owner whose key changed must be added again as a co-owner. The spec already says that a "suspected private-key compromise requires identity-key rotation and replacing grants for future content" (`wikistow.md` line 171), and the protocol left rotation, and its statement format, to a later milestone (`wikistow/protocol.md` section 4.6, Q4). This ADR decides the statement and what signs it, who can rotate, how contacts' apps take a rotation and tell it from a server swapping keys, what happens when someone else holds the old key too, agents, what old content stays readable under, whether shares rotate their keys, how the CLI and offline devices learn of it, and how it fits recovery, which keeps working.

In short:
- **Replace my keys**, in Settings → Security, for a person who still has their vault. Their app makes new keys at the next key version under the same identity ID, a new vault key, a new recovery key and a new passphrase, and signs out their other devices.
- **One statement, signed by both keys.** A **rotation statement** (`identity-key-rotation/v1`) names the old key by its key hash and holds the new public keys. The old key signs it, so apps that pinned the old key can follow; the new key signs it, so it proves the new key's holder made it and vouches for the old key as its predecessor.
- **Contacts' apps follow it without a warning.** An app that pinned the old key checks the chain of statements from its pin to the new key and moves the pin, with a notice, not the key-change warning. A server alone can't make a statement, so a substitution still shows as a key change. A thief who holds the old key and the person's session, or the server's help, can rotate too; that is a new accepted risk, A15.
- **The old key only opens.** The person's keyring keeps its earlier secret keys, to open what was sealed to them. Everyone verifies what the old key signed before. Nothing is sealed to it again, and a key version sealed with it doesn't count for writing.
- **Shares rotate their keys.** Whoever held the old key holds every folder key sealed to it. The person's app rotates every share of every space they own at once. Owners elsewhere are offered **Rotate keys and give access**, in one step.
- **A rotated owner is added again.** A rotation doesn't carry ownership: as after any key change, a co-owner adds the person again as an owner at the new key. Where no owner a member's app still trusts is left to do that, as in a space the person alone owns, that app accepts the new key as the owner through the chain.
- **Agents carry over if the person ticks them.** The app signs their enrollments again under the new key, for the agents the person keeps, and revokes every other agent of the account. The agents a person's app runs are made again, as their keys were sealed to the old key.
- **The CLI and offline devices** follow when they next reach the server: the CLI from its person's contacts record, a device by locking and asking for the new passphrase, as after a passphrase change.
- **Recovery doesn't change.** The new recovery key opens the new vault. It is how someone back from a lost passphrase gets in, and then they can replace their keys.

## What the code supports today

- **One key version per person, for good.** The server registers a person's identity only at key version 1 (`createVault`, `packages/server/src/content/vault.ts` line 44), and nothing else adds a human row; the only other insert is an agent's (`packages/server/src/content/agents.ts` line 443). The table could hold more: one row per `(id, keyVersion)` (`packages/server/src/db/schema/identities.ts` line 35), with at most one live human row per account (lines 39 to 41).
- **Recovery keeps the keys.** The recovery page opens the vault with the recovery key, then asks for a new passphrase (`packages/ui/src/routes/_app/vault/recover.tsx` lines 24 to 28 and 77 to 107). `PUT /api/vault` replaces the passphrase wrapper and nothing else: the keyring and the recovery wrapper must come back byte for byte (`packages/contracts/src/routes/vault.ts` lines 24 to 33, `packages/server/src/content/vault.ts` lines 117 to 148). So protocol section 4.6's "a person's key version still increases when they replace lost keys during recovery" describes a path that was never built. A person who loses both their passphrase and their recovery key can't make a new vault either (`vault-exists`, line 69). Today a higher key version is only ever a server's substitution.
- **The keyring holds one identity.** `{public, boxSecretKey, signSecretKey}` (`packages/client/src/vault.ts` line 57), under the vault key with the AD `vault-keyring/v1` (line 82).
- **The directory serves current keys only.** `lookupIdentities` returns each ID's newest live row (`packages/server/src/content/identities.ts` lines 39 to 52), and a request's caller is the account's newest live identity (lines 19 to 36 and 74 to 78). No route serves an earlier row.
- **One pin per person.** `TrustStore` keeps one key per identity ID (`packages/client/src/trust.ts` line 172). `require`, which every signature check uses, throws `unknown-identity` for any other key version (lines 265 to 271), so once a pin moves, what the person signed with the earlier key stops verifying. That is stricter than protocol section 8.4's "key changed" mark for a record above the pin. `confirmKey` replaces the pin after the key-change warning (lines 345 to 382). A pin's `method` is a strict enum of `first-seen` and `confirmed` (`packages/client/src/contacts.ts` line 73), and the interface shows a confirmed pin as checked (`packages/ui/src/sharing/people.ts` line 116).
- **Envelopes are served at the current key only.** Owner-region envelopes (`packages/server/src/content/serving.ts` line 76), folder key envelopes (`packages/server/src/content/sharing.ts` lines 1190 and 1226) and comment envelopes (`packages/server/src/content/comments.ts` line 548) are filtered by the caller's current key version.
- **Agents are bound to their person's key version.** `verifyEnrollment` requires the enrollment's `ownerKeyVersion` to be the person's (`trust.ts` line 97). A contacts record must be at its owner's key version (`contacts.ts` line 469), and only agents whose enrollment verifies under the owner's key get its content key (protocol section 8.1). The CLI's key file keeps the person at the key it pinned at enrollment (`apps/cli/src/keyfile.ts` lines 72 and 73).
- **Owner statements bind a key version** (protocol section 6.5: "An owner whose key version changes is accepted again only when an owner re-adds them"; the server's check, `packages/server/src/content/owners.ts` lines 329 to 336). Owner rows are by identity ID (`space_owners`), so the server's owner list doesn't change with a key.
- **The first owner of a space accepts itself** only if its first name record "verifies under `I`'s current key" (protocol section 6.5, step 1).
- **The kept wrapper moves before any unlock.** Every online read of the vault keeps the wrapper served (`vaultWrapper`, `packages/client/src/operations/vault.ts` lines 114 to 117), and a moved vault version forgets the remembered unlock at once (`restore`, `packages/ui/src/vault/open.ts` lines 178 to 181). The check of decision 4 has to come before both.
- **What's sealed to a person's box key.** Every folder key envelope and owner-region envelope addressed to them, their contacts record's private part, which holds the secret keys and tokens of the agents their app runs (protocol section 9.8), and everything a device keeps sealed for them, whose key comes from the box secret key (`packages/client/src/device-seal.ts` lines 11 and 12): kept pages, the search index and queued changes (ADR 0009).
- **A key change today** stops sharing with the person (`identity-key-changed`), makes them wait in shares that groups bring them into, and needs an owner's **Give access** at the new key (protocol sections 7.3, 7.9 and 8.4).

## Decisions

### 1. The rotation statement, and what signs it

A rotation statement says "the key at version `k` of this identity is replaced by these keys at `k + 1`".

```
IdentityRotation = { installationId, id: HumanId,
                     from: { keyVersion, keyHash },
                     to:   { keyVersion, boxPublicKey, signPublicKey },
                     contacts: { version, contactsHash } | null,
                     fromSignature, toSignature }

bytes = canonical('identity-key-rotation/v1', [installationId, id, from.keyVersion, from.keyHash,
        to.keyVersion, to.boxPublicKey (bytes), to.signPublicKey (bytes),
        contacts.version | null, contacts.contactsHash | null])
```

`from` is an identity reference without its ID (protocol section 4.4), so it binds the exact old keys. `to` holds the new public keys themselves, so a contact checks them against the statement, not against the directory. `to.keyVersion` is `from.keyVersion + 1`, and each new key differs from the old one of its kind. `contacts` names the last version of the person's contacts record signed with the old key, so nobody holding the old key can add versions after it that a device which knows of the rotation would take (the first review's M31-3). A chain of statements leads from any earlier key to the current one, one version at a time, and never comes back to a key it left. The domain is new: `identity-rotation/v1`, from the drafts before the folder-key design, was cut (protocol section 15), and isn't used again.

**What signs it.**
- **A. The old key only.** That is what lets a contact's app follow. But nothing shows the new key's holder agreed: whoever has the old key could name any keys, someone else's included.
- **B. The new key only.** It proves the new keys are held, but says nothing to someone who pinned the old key, which is the point.
- **C. Both, over the same bytes** (recommended). The old key's signature is the continuity for apps that pinned it. The new key's proves its holder agreed to the statement, and to the box key it names (neither signature proves anyone holds a box secret key, so the authority to rotate follows the old signing key alone, S31-11), and vouches for the old key as its predecessor, so someone who first met the person at the new key can still verify what they signed before. Each signature is checked under a different key, and the keys must differ, so neither stands in for the other. Decided (question 1).

**No reason, and no time.** The statement doesn't say why. A "routine" rotation would let an honest person skip rotating shares, but every rotation here is for a key that may have leaked (decision 6), and a thief's statement could claim anything. It has no signed time either: an app shows when it followed. Decided (question 3).

### 2. Who can rotate, and what changes

**Only the person, from an unlocked vault.** Rotating needs the old secret keys to sign, and an app with a session. Settings → Security → **Replace my keys** asks for a recent sign-in, with the second factor where it's on, as a passphrase change does. A person who has lost their vault can't rotate: they recover first, with the recovery key.

**What the app makes.**
- New X25519 and Ed25519 keys at `k + 1`.
- **A new vault key, recovery key and passphrase.** A leak of the keys may be a leak of the vault key too, from an unlocked device or served JavaScript (A8, A9), and then the old wrapper's copies open anything sealed under that vault key. So the new keyring goes under a new vault key, wrapped by a new passphrase and a new recovery key, shown once to save, as at setup. `wikistow.md` line 171 asks for exactly this: "Distinguish wrapper replacement from rotation after compromise." Decided (question 4).
- **The keyring keeps the earlier keys**, as `retired` (decision 5). The wrapper is format 2, with the keyring under a new AD, `vault-keyring/v2`, so an older app refuses it and asks to update (decision 10), and a server that relabels it format 1 gets a keyring that doesn't open, not one read without its earlier keys (E31-F2).
- The statement, signed by both keys.
- The agents' enrollments, signed again (decision 7).

**One request.** `POST /api/me/identity/rotate` carries the statement, the new public identity, the new wrapper, the vault version it replaces, the first contacts version signed with the new key, the kept agents' enrollments, and the agents kept and revoked, which between them must be every live agent of the account. Under a lock on the account, the server checks that `from` is the caller's current row, that no statement from that key version exists, that both signatures verify, that the statement's `contacts` is the caller's current contacts record, that the new keys pass protocol section 4.1 and belong to no other row, and that the wrapper's settings pass. In one transaction it adds the new row, keeps the old one as no longer current, stores the statement, the wrapper and the contacts version, stores the enrollments and revokes the other agents, ends the account's other sessions, and ends the live sessions in the spaces the person owns or is a member of (ADR 0015, decision 12). It records `identity.rotated`, and from then on refuses anything signed at the old key version.

**Ending other sessions.** A stolen device is often why someone rotates. Signing out every other device stops that one reaching the server, and the person's own devices sign in again. It doesn't end agents' credentials: the agents the person keeps carry on. The dialog also offers to change the account password, off by default, since a thief who knows it would sign in again and fetch what is sealed to the old key (M33-6); but a password change ends every agent's credential (protocol section 9.7), so the kept agents would then need enrolling again. Decided (question 4).

**Then the app finishes**, space by space, and again at each unlock until it's done, as a transfer's rotations are (protocol section 7.6): the rotation of every share in the spaces it owns (decision 6), and the new versions sealed to its kept agents. It shows the rotation as unfinished until then. It signs no owner statement and reviews none of the owners its contacts record accepts (question 9, narrowed): in a space with other owners, a co-owner adds the person again as an owner (decision 6). The dialog names those spaces, and says that until then their shares pause for everyone but the person.

**The first statement wins.** The server keeps one statement per key version. If someone else rotated first, the request fails, and the app explains below (decision 4).

### 3. How a contact's app takes it

When an app holding a pin sees a higher key version for the same ID (in the directory, on a record or an envelope, or on its own person's contacts record), it fetches the statements from the pinned version up and checks the chain: the first names the pin by key hash, each next one starts where the last ended, both signatures of each verify, and the last ends at the key it saw.

- **A. Follow it, with a notice** (recommended). The pin moves to the new key, keeping its label, in the next contacts write, as not checked (`first-seen`): nobody compared the new key, so the interface doesn't show it as checked (E32-L5). The app says once, where the person appears, that they replaced their keys, signed with their earlier key, and the contact's details list both fingerprints. Records the person signs at the new key verify, and their agents keep working. What is left is for owners, whose apps alone can do it anyway: giving them access at the new key where they are a member, one action with no key-change warning, and adding them again as an owner where they own a space with others (decision 6). Following is a confirmed contacts write: until the record holds it, the app only opens with the new key, so a write the server drops can't let a later launch follow another branch unnoticed (S31-7).
- **B. Ask each contact first.** A calmer notice than the key-change warning, with one button to accept. But a click without checking with the person adds no safety: the only thing a contact could check is the fingerprint, by phone or in person, which is today's warning. And until each contact clicks, owner-region saves stop (`unaccepted-owner`) and the person's records show unverified.
- **C. Treat it as a key change**, as today. Then a rotation brings no less friction than a substitution, which is what #327 asks for.

Following trusts the new key exactly as far as the app trusted the old one: whoever held the old key could already sign and seal as the person. What A adds for a thief is decision 4. Decided (question 2).

**A chain that doesn't check out** (a statement missing, a signature failing, a key that doesn't match, two statements for one key version, a key in `removed` along it) is a key change, with today's warning, and the app reports a possible attack. **A fork**, a second key at a version the app already followed, or another device's contacts record pinning a key off the chain, is a substitution (`identity-key-changed`) or a contacts conflict, never followed.

**Not them?** The notice offers to decline. Declining puts the followed key, and any later key on its chain, into `removed`, moves the pin back, and records the decline in the contacts record (`wikistow.declined`), so on every device no later key of theirs is followed and nothing is sealed to the restored pin; sharing with the person stops until a key is confirmed, as after any key change, and the app never follows a chain through those keys again.

**Telling a rotation from a server swapping keys.** The server can't sign with keys it doesn't hold, so it can't make a statement, and its substitution stays a key change with the warning. That is the distinction the issue asks for. The thing a statement can't tell apart is a rotation by the person from one by someone holding their old key (decision 4).

### 4. When someone else holds the old key too

What a thief can do with the old secret keys, by what else they have:
- **The keys alone.** They can't post a rotation without the person's session and a recent sign-in. They can already open everything sealed to the old key that they can get from the server, which needs the server's help (A3). One thing needs neither: with a device's kept contacts record (ADR 0009), the old key opens its private part, which holds the tokens of the agents the person's app runs, and those act as the agents, within their grants, until the person rotates, which revokes them (M31-7).
- **The keys and the person's session, or the server's help, before the person rotates.** They can rotate first, to keys of their own, with a vault wrapper of their own, so the person's passphrase and recovery key no longer open what the server serves. Contacts follow, and their key wins. Each of the person's devices keeps the public key it last unlocked with, unsealed, as its anchor, which only its own unlock or rotation moves, and never down: an older key or keyring the server serves it later is a rollback, and replaces nothing it kept (S32-5, E34-F1). When it sees the vault's version moved, it checks the directory's current key and its chain against that anchor first, which are public, before it asks for the new passphrase or drops what it kept. A valid chain from its own key to one it doesn't hold means the keys were replaced elsewhere: it says when, and asks whether the person did it. Before it asks, the device records the new key, so it signs nothing whatever the server serves later until it unlocks at that key (S37-2, S38-3). **I didn't** keeps the old wrapper and any remembered unlock, opens the vault with them where it can, tells the person to warn the people they share with in person, and signs and seals nothing more (M31-4). A browser that keeps nothing has no anchor and takes the server's word, and a server helping the thief can keep serving the person's devices the old wrapper and key; both are in A15. Those people can compare fingerprints, decline the key, and remove the identity from their shares. Nothing in the protocol takes the identity back. A way to start again with a new identity, which a person who lost both their passphrase and recovery key needs too, is for another issue. Decided (question 7).
- **The keys and the server's help, after the person rotated.** The server can show an app that hasn't followed yet a chain of the thief's instead, so that app follows the thief. Each app sees one chain and can't know of the other. This goes unnoticed unless two people compare fingerprints, as at first sight (A4). The thief can also still sign records, envelopes, owner statements and contacts versions with the old key. Apps that followed read such records as the person's earlier writing, but never take a key to write under, an owner or an agent from the old key (decision 5), so nothing new is sealed where the thief can read it.
- **What the old key wrote into the contacts record before the rotation stays**: the first version at the new key extends it, so a pin the thief moved before it, or an owner they added in any space, the spaces the person owns included, carries over (M34-1, M34-2). Nothing reviews it: the review of owners in the spaces the person owns went with the narrowing (question 9). Reviewing the record would be a new mechanism; it is in A15.
- **A server helping them can hide the rotation from others**, showing the old key as current, so an owner's apps seal new versions to it until they meet the new key (S34-1). Only the server says which key is current.
- **Everything the old key opened stays open to them** (A3): every folder key version sealed to it, so what members save, and type in live sessions, in a share until an owner rotates it (the rotation ends only the sessions open at that moment, S31-6), the owner-region content keys, and the private part of the contacts record as it was (labels, plugin approvals, and the keys and tokens of the agents the app runs).

The ADR records these as accepted risk **A15** (protocol section 2.4). A stronger answer is an offline key that alone may rotate, such as one derived from the recovery key, which contacts would pin at first sight. That needs every existing identity to commit to it first, signed by a key that may already have leaked, and is listed under **Considered options**.

### 5. What stays readable, and under what

**Content keys never change**, so every record, published version, comment, history entry and public link reads as before. The question is only whose keys open and verify them.

**The person's own app** keeps every earlier secret key in its keyring (`retired`), and opens envelopes addressed to any key version it holds, current or earlier. The server serves a person their envelopes at every key version of theirs, not only the current one. It never seals or signs with an earlier key. What a device sealed for itself under an earlier key it opens once, to seal again under the new key: kept pages, the search index and queued changes.
- **A. Keep the earlier secret keys** (recommended). Nothing is re-sealed. The keys are the leaked ones, so keeping them exposes nothing more.
- **B. Re-seal everything to the new key**, then drop the old. Every envelope and wrap the person holds, in one request or many, the size of adding a co-owner to every space at once (Q8), for history whoever stole the key holds anyway. Decided (question 6).

**Every app**, the person's own included for its retired keys (M32-2), verifies what the person signed with an earlier key through the chain: the directory serves earlier rows by key version, checked against the chain's key hashes. A key reached through the chain before the pin is an **earlier key** of that person. Its signatures verify for opening, with no mark. It counts for nothing else:
- a key version whose envelope it sealed doesn't count for writing (protocol section 6.5, **Writing**), with the one exception that a rotation may verify the current version through it (decision 6);
- an owner statement it signed accepts nobody (section 6.5, step 3);
- nothing is sealed to it, and an agent's enrollment under it counts only to verify what that agent wrote, for agents the contacts record named by the rotation's cutoff (S32-7, M33-7);
- a contacts version it signed counts only up to the version the rotation statement names, and once an app knows of the rotation, the record it takes as current is signed with the current key: key versions along a contacts chain never go down.

So once an app follows, a thief signing with the old key can show it old-looking records, but can't make it write under keys the thief knows. History stays readable after the person rotates, and the published versions, which readers open with the share's published keys, don't depend on the person's keys at all.

### 6. Shares and ownership

**Shares rotate their keys.** Whoever holds the old key holds every folder key ever sealed to it, so content saved later in those shares is theirs too, with the server's help, until a new key version leaves them out. Leaving rotation to owners' discretion, as after an agent's revocation (protocol section 9.7), would leave every share the person was in open by default.
- **In the spaces the person owns**, their app rotates every share at once, after the rotation lands (decision 2), in one dialog that lists each share's remaining people, as protocol section 7.5 does, and raises its **No going back** marks. Its own envelope of each new version goes to its new key. Until then, members' apps that followed see the current version sealed by an earlier key, which doesn't count for writing, and wait for the new version: a pause, not a leak. In a space with other owners the pause lasts until a co-owner adds the person again as an owner (below), since until then no other app opens what the new key seals.
- **In spaces others own**, no rotation seals a new version to the person's earlier key: every rotation names a member whose key was replaced in `rekeyed`, `waiting` or `removed`, and the server refuses anything else, and no give without a rotation reaches them (M33-1, M33-2). The person waits at their new key, as a member whose key changed does (protocol section 7.9), and still reads what they had through their earlier keys. The owners' apps list them under **Waiting** as having replaced their keys, not with the key-change warning, and offer **Rotate keys and give access** for each share they're in: one rotation whose new version goes to everyone remaining, with every earlier version sealed to the person's new key in the same request. The app offers it for a share whose current version it verified sealed to the person's earlier key, by an owner it writes with, taking their role from that envelope, or, for someone waiting or whose envelope came only from an owner's earlier key, once the owner ticks them, as a reader unless raised (M34-4): a chain shows the person is the same, not that they belong in the share, so the server's **Waiting** row alone never brings anyone in (S31-4). The person's agents get the new versions from the person once they have them.

**Shares left behind.** A share whose current version only an earlier key sealed, because its owner hasn't finished rotating or removed themself before, holds every app that followed: none writes there. Owners' apps list such shares and offer **Rotate keys**, as after a revoked agent (M32-6).

**A rotated owner is added again.** A rotation doesn't carry ownership. An app that follows an owner's rotation keeps them accepted at their earlier keys, for opening, and doesn't accept the new key as an owner: as after any key change today, a co-owner adds them again (protocol section 7.6), sealing everything to the new key and signing a statement naming it, which every app that accepts that co-owner then follows. Until then the other owners' apps refuse to seal in the space (`unaccepted-owner`), name the person and offer to add them again, and nobody else opens or writes under what the person seals with the new key.
- **A. Added again by a co-owner** (decided on 2026-11-24). No owner statement is signed again for the rotation, and the person's app reviews no owners: what the old key wrote into the contacts record stays (A15), as it does for pins. The cost: #327's "a rotating owner stays an owner" isn't met by the rotation itself, a space with other owners pauses until one of them acts, and an app that works out a space's owners afresh reaches the owners the person added with the old key only through other owners' statements, since an earlier key's statement accepts nobody.
- **B. Followed through the chain** (decided on 2026-11-23, superseded). Apps accepted the owner at each later key on a chain they follow. But apps reached other owners through the person's statements, which the old key's holder could sign too, so the person's app signed them again with the new key, after a review of every owner its record accepted. Six review rounds found Highs in that review each time.

**Adding them again** is section 7.6's re-add. The adder's app records the new key before it gathers the keys, so it opens what the person sealed with it, and then rotates every share whose current version was sealed to the person's earlier key (M40-2). The server refuses a rotated owner removing another owner, or adding a co-owner, while the space lists an owner at the key version they were added at, so a thief who rotated first can't remove the co-owners who would have to add them again (M40-1). A co-owner who doubts the rotation removes the person instead; their app then records the new key for opening only, as for any removed owner, and the person's envelopes add nobody to the new versions (M40-4). A member's app that holds a version the new key sealed, which it can't use yet, raises its **No going back** mark to it, so it stops writing under the version the old key holds (S40-2).

**When no owner a member's app trusts is left**, as when the person alone owns the space, every owner it accepted there rotated, or the others left, no co-owner can add anyone again, and its members could never again open what is sealed there. So that app accepts each owner it accepted there at the latest key of a chain it follows, and records it; step 3 then runs through their new statements. Owners' apps always accept themselves, so they never meet this case, and agents take it from their person's record. A server that lists only rotated owners of a space with others gets the same, with no co-owner acting (A15). A removed owner stays removed, as `removedOwners` holds IDs. An app accepts itself as a space's first owner when its first name record verifies under any key its keyring holds, not only the current one, and records itself at the new key. New owner-region saves seal to the sealing owners at the keys the server lists. Older owner-region records stay sealed to their earlier key, which their keyring opens.

**A rotation from a version an earlier key sealed.** When a share's current version was sealed with the earlier key of an accepted owner, which will be every share the rotating owner rotated last, that version doesn't count for writing, so a rotation from it would be refused. In a rotation, and in an owner's removal, envelopes sealed with an earlier key of an accepted owner who isn't removed count when verifying that version, as protocol section 7.6, step 2, does for an owner being removed. They don't move the **No going back** mark, and **they add nobody to the remaining people**: whoever holds the old key could have made them, for anyone (the first review's M31-1). So the dialog lists the members whose keys came only from the earlier key by their labels, unticked, and seals to those the owner ticks; the rest wait for **Give access**, kept as members (`waiting`), not removed (S32-6). Giving them access later is a new grant: an owner's app that can't verify them through an envelope of the current version by an owner it writes with says so, whatever the server reports, and shows the server's reason for their waiting (M37-4, S38-2). Their role is reader unless the owner raises it, as the earlier key's envelope could claim any role (S31-1). An owner who removes a rotated owner instead of adding them again treats what that owner sealed with their new key the same way, since nobody vouched for that key as an owner. In such a share that is every member, since the rotating owner sealed the whole version: the owner ticks the people they know belong there, and a removed member the server slipped back into the list is the one they leave out.

Decided (question 3).

### 7. Agents enrolled under the old key

An agent's own keys don't change, and its ID derives from its person's ID and its keys (protocol section 4.2), not their key version. But its enrollment is signed at the old key version, and nothing it can verify says the new key is its person's.

- **The agents the person ticks carry over.** The rotation dialog lists every live agent of the account, by its label, the day it was enrolled and its agent ID, none ticked, and asks the person to check the ID with `wikistow whoami` on its machine: before the rotation the old key could rename a thief's agent after a real one (M32-4). For each one the person ticks, the app signs the enrollment again with the new key: the same agent, at `ownerKeyVersion` `k + 1`, with no fingerprint to compare, since the agent is already the person's. Every other agent is revoked, which ends its credential and grants: the request names every live agent as kept or revoked, and the server refuses it otherwise, so an agent the server knows of but the contacts record doesn't list can't outlive the rotation. An agent the server lists that the contacts record doesn't is never offered. A thief who enrolled an agent of their own with the old key shows up here, unticked, under a label or a day the person doesn't know (M31-2).
- **The agents the person's app runs** (protocol section 9.8) are revoked, and made again the next time the page chat needs one. Their secret keys and tokens were in the private part of the contacts record, sealed to the old key.
- **An agent follows its person.** It finds its person's contacts record at the new key, follows the chain from the key it pinned at enrollment, and keeps the new key in its local file. It accepts its enrollment only signed again at its person's current key, so an agent the person dropped can't read the record. What its person sealed to it with the earlier key it opens, but writes only under versions its person seals with the new key, which the person's app does as it finishes the rotation, for the shares it then holds.

Re-enrolling every agent from scratch would be the alternative: no new rule, but every CLI's user would go through the lookup code and fingerprint again. Decided (question 5).

### 8. How the CLI and offline devices learn of it

- **The CLI** reads its person's contacts record at each command, as now. A record at a higher key version makes it fetch the chain from the key it pinned, check it, write the new key to its local file with its contacts mark (protocol section 9.3), and say once on standard error that its person replaced their keys, signed with their earlier key. A chain that doesn't check out stops it with `identity-key-changed`, and it tells the user to enroll again. An agent whose person dropped it can't open the record, and its credential is ended anyway.
- **A device that was offline** keeps unlocking offline with the wrapper it kept, as before (A13): offline it can't know. Back online, the server's wrapper version has moved. Before anything else the app checks the directory's key and chain against its anchor (decision 4); for a rotation this person made elsewhere it then locks and asks for the new passphrase, as it does after a passphrase change made elsewhere (ADR 0009, decision 5). The new keyring opens what the device kept under the old key, which it seals again under the new one, and its queued changes are signed and sealed with the new key when sent, as offline changes always are. A device the rotation signed out signs in first.
- **Other people's devices** follow the chain the first time they meet the new key, online.

### 9. The recovery path

It keeps working as built (protocol section 5.6): the recovery key opens the vault, and the person chooses a new passphrase. After a rotation, the new recovery key is the one that works. An old recovery key opens only a copy of the old wrapper, if anyone kept one, and that holds only the old keys, which are the leaked ones. Recovery and rotation are separate on purpose: recovery is for a lost passphrase, rotation for a leaked key, and someone who lost a device may need both, recovery first.

The protocol's claim that recovery raises the key version (section 4.6) is corrected to what's built: it never does.

### 10. Older apps and servers

- **The person's own older apps** refuse a format 2 wrapper, rather than run with a keyring whose earlier keys they can't see. Today's apps refuse it only as an answer they can't read ("Try again in a moment"), since the wrapper schema takes format 1 alone, which could send someone to recovery with a key that no longer works. So a release before rotation ships teaches them to say the wrapper needs an update (E32-L3).
- **Other people's older apps** see a key change, with today's warning. Confirming it is what they do today, and an owner among them adds the person again as a co-owner, as current apps' owners do. An older member's app in a space the person alone owns doesn't accept the new key as an owner, and pauses there until updated. An older owner's app can't rotate a share holding a member who rotated, or give them access, as the server refuses what it would send; it says so, and to update (M34-7). They keep working once updated.
- **An older server** has no rotate route, so **Replace my keys** says the server needs an update.

### 11. Routes

| Request | What the server does |
|---|---|
| `POST /api/me/identity/rotate {rotation, identity, wrapper, expectedVaultVersion, contacts, enrollments, keptAgents, revokedAgents}` | Decision 2. Behind a recent sign-in. |
| `GET /api/identities/:id/rotations?from=n` | The statements of that identity whose `from.keyVersion` is `n` or more, in order, to anyone signed in who may look the identity up. |
| `GET /api/identities/:id/versions/:keyVersion` | An earlier row's public keys. |
| `POST /api/shares/:shareId/rotate` | Gains `rekeyed`: members at a new key whom this rotation also gives every earlier version, as **Give access** does; and `waiting`: members the rotation leaves out without removing them, who keep their rows and grants and wait (decision 6). |

Serving envelopes at every key version of the caller changes the existing routes, not their shapes.

### Out of scope

- **Starting again with a new identity**, for a person who lost both their passphrase and their recovery key, or whose identity someone else rotated (decision 4, question 7).
- **Rotating an agent's keys.** That is enrolling a new agent, as today (protocol section 4.2).
- **Rotating on a schedule.** Every rotation costs owners a step; it's for a key that may have leaked.

## Considered options

- **Re-adding the person everywhere, as today.** No new format, but every owner sees the key-change warning and adds them again as a co-owner, for a change the person made on purpose (decision 3, C). Chosen for owners only, without the warning (decision 6, A).
- **Owners followed through the chain**, with the person's app signing owner statements again and reviewing the owners it accepts (decision 6, B; the scope decided on 2026-11-23).
- **Only the old key signs** (decision 1, A), **or only the new** (decision 1, B).
- **A rotation key held offline**, derived from the recovery key or saved separately, which alone could sign a rotation, as Matrix's cross-signing master key or Keybase's paper keys work. It is the one design that tells the person from a thief holding the identity key. But contacts would have to pin it at first sight, every existing identity would commit to it with a key that may already have leaked, and the recovery key would gain a second job that makes it worth more to steal. It can come later, as a statement format of its own, if A15 proves too wide.
- **A waiting period** before contacts follow, so the person could object. The server decides what each app sees and when, so a wait proves nothing against it.
- **Asking each contact to accept** (decision 3, B).
- **Re-sealing history to the new key** (decision 5, B).
- **A "routine" rotation** that rotates no shares (decision 1).
- **Re-enrolling every agent** (decision 7).
- **Keeping the vault key**, and replacing only the keyring under it. One fewer secret to save, but whoever had the vault key opens the new keyring from the server's copy.

Scott accepted every recommendation below on 2026-11-23. On 2026-11-24 he changed his answer to question 9, which changes questions 2 and 3 where they reach owners, as their notes say.

1. **One statement, `identity-key-rotation/v1`, signed by both the old key and the new?** Recommended: yes (decision 1). The old key's signature lets contacts follow; the new key's shows its holder agreed and vouches for the old key, so history signed before stays verifiable by anyone who meets the person later.
2. **Contacts' apps follow a valid chain without asking, with a notice?** Recommended: yes (decision 3, A). The only real check a contact can make is the fingerprint, which is today's key-change warning; a click that checks nothing adds no safety, and until it came, owner-region saves would stop. A server alone still can't move a pin. The cost is A15: a thief with the old key and the person's session, or the server's help, is followed too.
   *Changed by question 9 (2026-11-24):* the pin follows as decided, but an owner's acceptance no longer does, except in a space the person alone owns (decision 6). So in a space with other owners, owner-region saves do stop until a co-owner adds the person again.
3. **Every rotation counts as a leak: the person's own shares rotate at once, and owners elsewhere get "Rotate keys and give access" as one step?** Recommended: yes (decisions 1 and 6). The old key holds every folder key sealed to it. A "routine" rotation that skipped this would be the one option a thief would always pick. The cost: in a share whose current version the rotating owner sealed, the new version goes only to the members the owner ticks, since whoever holds the old key could have sealed that version to anyone.
   *Changed by question 9 (2026-11-24):* the person's shares still rotate at once, but in a space with other owners nobody else opens the new versions until a co-owner adds the person again, so those shares pause until then (decision 6).
4. **A rotation makes a new vault key, recovery key and passphrase, and signs out the person's other devices; and does it change the account password too?** Recommended: the first, yes (decision 2): a leaked identity key may come with the vault key or the passphrase, and the device it leaked from may be one of the person's; the cost is a new recovery key to save, and signing in again elsewhere. For the password, recommended: offer it in the dialog, off by default. A thief who knows it signs in again and fetches what the old key already opens, but no new version is sealed to that key; changing it ends every agent's credential (protocol section 9.7), so every kept agent would need enrolling again.
5. **Agents carry over, with their enrollments signed again, when the person ticks them, and every other agent of the account is revoked; the page chat's agents are made again?** Recommended: yes (decision 7). Nothing ticked by default, so an agent a thief enrolled isn't kept by a click past it; re-enrolling every CLI by hand is the alternative.
6. **The keyring keeps the earlier secret keys, to open history, rather than re-sealing history to the new key?** Recommended: keep them (decision 5). They are the leaked keys, so keeping them costs nothing, where re-sealing is the size of adding a co-owner to every space.
7. **A rotation someone else made first is not undone by the protocol: the person's app says so and stops, and a way to start again with a new identity is a separate issue?** Recommended: yes (decision 4, and A15). Undoing it needs a key the thief doesn't hold, such as an offline rotation key (**Considered options**), which every identity would have to commit to in advance.
8. **Older apps of the rotated person refuse the new wrapper and ask to update, which needs a release that teaches them so before rotation ships?** Recommended: yes (decision 10). The alternative, ignoring the earlier keys, leaves them unable to open history and to accept themselves as owners, with errors that don't say why.

## Decided while designing the narrowed scope (2026-11-24)

These were decided without Scott, where the narrowing left a gap. Each can be changed.

- **Where no trusted owner is left, members follow.** In a space the person alone owns, or where every owner a member's app accepted has rotated or left, no co-owner the member trusts can add anyone again, so its members would never again open what is sealed there, and the rotation of its shares (question 3) would cut them off for good. Their apps accept the new keys as owners through the chain instead (decision 6). This is what question 9's first answer did everywhere, kept only where the co-owner path can't run, and decided from what each member's app accepts, not from the server's list at one moment (round 40, E40-F2, E40-F3). It needs no review, since there is no trusted owner to reach. The alternative was to leave those members unable to open anything new, or to skip rotating those shares, which leaves the leaked key holding what members save there.
- **A rotated owner nobody added again can't remove co-owners** or add new ones, while an owner the server lists at the key version they were added at is there (round 40, M40-1). Otherwise someone who rotated first could remove the co-owners and leave the members following them as the only owner.
- **Removing a rotated owner instead of adding them again.** A co-owner who doesn't believe the rotation can remove the person as an owner (protocol section 7.6). What the person sealed with the new key then counts, in that removal, as an earlier key's envelopes do: for the writing check only, adding nobody to the new versions, with the members shown unticked (decision 6, protocol section 4.6, **Using an earlier key**). Nobody vouched for that key as an owner, so it mustn't choose who gets the new versions. The remover's app records the key for opening only, as for any removed owner, so later members and co-owners can still be given those versions (M40-4).
- **The added owner signs its adder.** When a co-owner adds the person again, the person's app names the adder, as a new co-owner's does, and once the person confirms, signs a statement naming them at the new key (protocol section 7.6). This is the co-owner path's own statement, not one signed again for the rotation, and it lets apps that accept the person at the new key reach that co-owner.
- **The person's own app records itself at the new key** in the spaces it owns, in the first contacts version at the new key, and drops its own ID from every `removedOwners` there, so an entry the old key wrote can't keep it out of its own spaces. It keeps accepting the other owners its record accepts, and reviews none (A15).
- **The rotation dialog names the spaces with other owners**, and says their shares pause for everyone but the person until a co-owner adds them again.
- **Owners the person added with the old key** are reached, by an app that works out a space's owners afresh after the rotation (a new member or co-owner), only through other owners' statements, since an earlier key's owner statement accepts nobody. Where none reaches them, that app neither opens nor writes under what they seal, and an owner's app can't seal in the space (`unaccepted-owner`). This is as after any key change today, and costs availability, not confidentiality; signing those statements again is what the narrowing dropped.
